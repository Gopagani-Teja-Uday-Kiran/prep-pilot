# OULAD: VLE Engagement and Student Outcomes

## Research Question
Does a student's overall activity on the Virtual Learning Environment (VLE) have any relationship with their final course result?

## Data and Methodology
For this analysis, I used 32,593 student enrollment records from the Open University Learning Analytics Dataset (OULAD).

First, I calculated the total number of VLE clicks made by each student for their respective course module and presentation. I then combined this information with the students' final assessment results.

To understand the relationship between activity and academic performance, I divided students into four groups based on their total number of clicks and compared the results of each group.

## Key Findings
The results showed a noticeable difference between students with lower and higher VLE activity.

Among students in the lowest-click group, 70.8% withdrew from their courses. In comparison, students in the highest-click group had better outcomes: 65.0% passed, 21.2% achieved a distinction, and only 4.6% withdrew.

I also noticed that withdrawal rates generally decreased as VLE activity increased across individual course modules.

## Limitations
Although the results show a clear relationship between VLE activity and student outcomes, they do not prove that increased activity directly leads to better academic performance.

The analysis considers total clicks recorded throughout the entire course. It does not show when students were active or whether their activity was consistent. Additionally, the number of students in each click group varied across modules, which may affect the comparisons.

## Conclusion and Future Work
Overall, the analysis suggests that students who interacted more frequently with the VLE generally achieved better final results in this dataset.

However, simply increasing the number of clicks does not necessarily mean that a student is learning more effectively.

As a next step, I would like to analyze student activity at different stages of the course, using only the clicks recorded before a particular point in time. This could help identify whether early engagement can provide useful insights into students' eventual academic performance.
